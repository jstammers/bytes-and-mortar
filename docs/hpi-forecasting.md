# HPI Forecasting Methodology

This document explains how the House Price Index (HPI) forecasting feature works,
what drives the UK HPI, and how to use the forecasting API and web interface.

---

## What is the UK House Price Index?

The UK House Price Index (HPI) is a monthly index measuring the change in residential
property prices across England, Wales, Scotland, and Northern Ireland. It is published
jointly by HM Land Registry and the ONS using a **hedonic regression model** applied to
completed transaction data. The base period is **January 2015 = 100**.

Because individual property transactions are volatile, the HPI is more reliable for
understanding market trends than looking at mean sale prices alone.

---

## Key factors driving UK HPI

House prices are driven by a combination of macroeconomic, demographic, and structural
forces operating at different time horizons.

### Short-term (< 1 year)

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| Bank of England base rate / mortgage rates | Strong negative: every 1 pp rise reduces affordability and demand |
| Consumer confidence & employment | Positive: higher confidence encourages purchases |
| Seasonal demand | Spring peaks (Mar-May), winter troughs (Nov-Jan) |
| Policy shocks | Stamp duty changes, Help-to-Buy extensions create sudden demand shifts |

### Medium-term (1-5 years)

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| Real household income growth | Positive; income elasticity of UK house prices is estimated > 1 |
| Net housing supply (completions vs. household formation) | Persistent undersupply exerts upward pressure |
| Credit availability | LTV limits and affordability stress tests directly constrain buyers |
| Net migration | Raises household formation, increasing demand |

### Long-term structural (5+ years)

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| Household formation rate | Number of households is the most reliable long-run predictor |
| Regional divergence | London and South East historically grow 1-2 pp/yr faster than the North |
| Income elasticity > 1 | Over the long run prices tend to outpace income growth |
| Construction costs | Rise in real building costs sets a floor on new-build pricing |

### What the literature says

Meen (1996) established a cointegration model showing that the long-run equilibrium of
UK house prices is driven by households, permanent income, and construction costs,
with prices reverting to this level after short-run disturbances.

More recent work (Huang et al., 2023) found that **Exponential Smoothing (ETS)
outperforms both ARIMA and ARIMA-Xreg** on the UK Residential Property Price Index,
and ensemble weighting across multiple models typically reduces MSE by ~37%
(Yusupova et al., 2023).

## Forecasting methods

Four methods are implemented in `src/models/hpi_forecast.py`. All share a common
`HPIForecaster` interface and return a `ForecastResult` containing a point forecast
and lower/upper prediction interval bounds.

### 1. ETS (Exponential Smoothing)

```
ETSForecaster(trend="add", seasonal="add", seasonal_periods=12)
```

Holt-Winters additive ETS fits a state-space model with separate level, trend,
and seasonal components, each with their own smoothing coefficient. Parameters are
estimated by maximum likelihood.

**Prediction intervals** are generated by simulating 2,000 sample paths using
bootstrapped innovations. This captures parameter uncertainty more accurately than
the closed-form Gaussian approximation, especially at multi-step horizons.

**Best for:** Monthly series of at least 3 full seasonal cycles (36 months).

**Evidence:** Outperforms ARIMA on UK RPPI in published benchmarks (Huang et al. 2023).

---

### 2. SARIMA (Seasonal ARIMA)

```
SARIMAForecaster(order=(1,1,1), seasonal_order=(1,1,1,12))
```

SARIMAX(p,d,q)(P,D,Q,12) models the non-seasonal autoregressive structure
`(p,d,q)` alongside the seasonal structure `(P,D,Q,12)`. First-differencing
(`d=1`, `D=1`) removes the unit root and seasonal unit root common in property
price indices.

**Prediction intervals** are Gaussian confidence intervals derived from the
state-space innovations variance.

**Auto-order selection** (`auto_order=True`) runs a grid search over
(p,d,q) in {0,1,2} x {0,1} x {0,1,2} and (P,D,Q) in {0,1} x {0,1} x {0,1},
selecting the combination with the lowest AIC.

**Best for:** At least 5 years of monthly data (60 points). Handles seasonality
explicitly and is highly interpretable.

---

### 3. Trend Extrapolation

```
TrendForecaster(degree=2, n_bootstrap=2000)
```

Fits a polynomial of the specified degree to the time index and computes residuals.
Prediction intervals are built by bootstrapping (resampling with replacement) those
residuals; uncertainty grows proportionally to `sqrt(h)` where `h` is the forecast
horizon, reflecting the increasing uncertainty of longer-range projections.

**No statsmodels dependency** - uses pure numpy. Suitable for:

- Short history (< 3 years)
- Quick baseline comparisons
- Situations where the ETS/SARIMA models fail to converge

---

### 4. Ensemble (Weighted Combination)

```
EnsembleForecaster(forecasters=[ETSForecaster(), SARIMAForecaster(), TrendForecaster()])
```

Combines the point forecasts of all components as a **weighted average**, where
weights are the Akaike weights derived from each model's AIC:

```
w_i = exp(-0.5 * (AIC_i - min(AIC))) / sum(exp(-0.5 * (AIC_j - min(AIC))))
```

If AIC is not available (e.g. TrendForecaster alone), uniform weights are used.

Prediction intervals are combined as the weighted average of each model's bounds
(`pi_method="weighted"`) or as the widest union (`pi_method="union"`).

**Evidence:** Ensemble methods consistently outperform single-model forecasts in
M-competition results. Expected MSE reduction: ~37% vs best single model.

## Prediction interval interpretation

All prediction intervals report `1 - alpha` nominal coverage:

| alpha | Coverage |
|---|---|
| 0.05 | 95% |
| 0.10 | 90% |
| 0.20 | 80% |

A **95% prediction interval** means that, if the model is well-specified, roughly
95% of future observations should fall within the interval. In practice, forecast
models make simplifying assumptions; treat the interval as a probabilistic guide
rather than a guarantee.

Key characteristics:

- **Width grows with horizon**: uncertainty compounds over time.
- **Asymmetry**: ETS and Trend forecasters may produce asymmetric intervals due
  to simulation; SARIMA intervals are symmetric (Gaussian).
- **Long-run drift**: polynomial trend intervals widen faster at long horizons
  than ETS/SARIMA due to the `sqrt(h)` scaling.

## Reliability: short-term vs long-term

| Horizon | Reliability | Recommended method | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1-3 months | High | Ensemble or ETS | Momentum and seasonality dominate |
| 3-12 months | Moderate | Ensemble | Macro factors start to matter more |
| 1-2 years | Moderate-low | Ensemble with caution | Interest rate uncertainty is key |
| 2-5 years | Low | Trend (structural view) | Policy and supply shocks are unpredictable |
| 5+ years | Very low | Do not rely on intervals | Long-run structural analysis only |

For horizon > 12 months, widen the interval mentally: model uncertainty, structural
breaks, and policy changes are not captured in any time-series model.

## API usage

### Available endpoints

```
GET  /api/hpi-forecast/regions          → list[str]
POST /api/hpi-forecast                  → HPIForecastResponse
```

### Forecast request

```http
POST /api/hpi-forecast
Content-Type: application/json

{
  "region": "City of Westminster",
  "method": "ensemble",
  "steps": 12,
  "alpha": 0.05
}
```

**Parameters:**

| Field | Type | Default | Description |
|---|---|---|---|
| `region` | string | required | District/region name (see `/api/hpi-forecast/regions`) |
| `method` | `"ets"`, `"sarima"`, `"trend"`, `"ensemble"` | `"ensemble"` | Forecasting method |
| `steps` | int 1-60 | `12` | Number of months to forecast |
| `alpha` | float 0.01-0.50 | `0.05` | Significance level (1-alpha = PI coverage) |

### Forecast response

```json
{
  "region": "City of Westminster",
  "method": "ensemble",
  "historical": [
    {"date": "2022-01-01", "value": 1150000.0},
    ...
  ],
  "forecast": [
    {
      "date": "2025-01-01",
      "point": 1280000.0,
      "lower": 1190000.0,
      "upper": 1380000.0
    },
    ...
  ]
}
```

### Python usage

```python
from src.models.hpi_forecast import EnsembleForecaster
import polars as pl

# Load cleaned HPI data for a region
df = pl.scan_parquet("data/raw/uk_hpi_full.parquet") \
       .filter(pl.col("regionname") == "England") \
       .sort("date") \
       .collect()

forecaster = EnsembleForecaster()
forecaster.fit_from_frame(df, value_col="averageprice")
result = forecaster.forecast(steps=24, alpha=0.05)

print(result.to_frame())
```

### Interpreting `ForecastResult`

```python
result.horizon          # number of forecast steps
result.point            # numpy array of central forecasts
result.lower            # numpy array of lower PI bounds
result.upper            # numpy array of upper PI bounds
result.coverage(actuals)  # empirical PI coverage (0.0 – 1.0)
result.to_frame()       # tidy Polars DataFrame
```

## Caveats and limitations

1. **Univariate only.** These methods model the HPI series alone; they do not
   incorporate macroeconomic covariates (interest rates, GDP, unemployment).
   Adding exogenous regressors (ARIMAX/VAR) would improve accuracy at medium horizons.

2. **Structural breaks.** Events like the 2008 financial crisis, COVID-19 pandemic,
   or sudden policy changes (stamp duty holidays) are not predictable from history.
   Post-break data should be weighted more heavily in practice.

3. **Regional heterogeneity.** A single national or regional forecast masks
   postcode-level variation. London and the South East are more volatile and
   carry higher uncertainty than Northern regions.

4. **Model misspecification.** If the data-generating process changes (e.g. a shift
   in housing policy), the model's prediction intervals will be too narrow.

5. **Seasonal periods.** These models assume a fixed 12-month seasonal pattern.
   If the seasonality changes (e.g. COVID disrupted the typical spring peak), the
   model may take several cycles to adapt.
